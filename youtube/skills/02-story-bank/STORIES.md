## STORY_NIT_EEE — NIT Calicut, EEE branch, 2018

**One-liner**: I did Electrical and Electronics Engineering at NIT Calicut. A non-IT branch. I'm proof you can pivot from anywhere.

### Framing A — "Non-IT to AI is possible"
> 2018. I graduated from NIT Calicut. EEE branch. Electrical and Electronics. Not even Computer Science — a non-IT branch. If you're watching this and you're from Mechanical, Civil, ECE, or any non-IT background and you're thinking "I'm too late, I'm in the wrong stream" — I started exactly where you are. Today I'm an AI engineer in Europe. So that excuse is gone.

### Framing B — "The branch doesn't decide your career"
> I did EEE at NIT Calicut. For 4 years I studied transformers — the *electrical* kind, not the AI kind. None of that helped me become an AI engineer. What helped was deciding, *after* college, that I'd learn whatever the market actually wanted. Your branch is a starting point, not a sentence.

### Framing C — "The college name doesn't save you either"
> NIT Calicut sounds prestigious. It's a Tier-1 college. And you know what my first job offered me? `3 LPA` at Infosys, on a dying technology stack. The college tag did nothing. What worked was building skills the market actually paid for. So if you're not from a Tier-1 — relax. It matters less than the internet tells you.

## STORY_INFOSYS_MAINFRAMES — First job, stuck in legacy

**One-liner**: First job at Infosys, on mainframes. `3 LPA`. I was lost. No future in that stack.

### Framing A — "Trapped"
> My first job was at Infosys. The technology I got assigned to: mainframes. Old. Dying. No company was building anything new on it. I'd open my laptop every morning and write code that I knew, in 5 years, no one would care about. If you're a working pro right now writing manual test cases, doing support tickets, or maintaining some 15-year-old Java app — I know exactly what that feels like. I lived it.

### Framing B — "Grateful but ready to move"
> I'm grateful to Infosys. They gave me my first salary. They taught me how a real engineering team operates — code reviews, deployment processes, on-call. But the *technology* — mainframes — had no future. Two truths can exist at once. You can be grateful to your current company AND know it's time to move. That's how I learned to think about career.

### Framing C — "I had no roadmap"
> I knew I had to leave mainframes. But leave for *what*? Machine learning was just becoming a thing. I watched 50 YouTube videos. Every video told me a different starting point. Statistics first. No, Python first. No, do Andrew Ng's course. No, start with Kaggle. I was completely lost. That confusion is exactly why this channel exists — so you don't waste 6 months on the wrong path the way I did.

## STORY_WB_DT_SWISS — Production journey across global companies

**One-liner**: After Infosys: Warner Bros & Discovery → Deutsche Telekom → now Switzerland's largest telecom.

### Framing A — "Authority on production"
> When I tell you a particular RAG pattern breaks at scale, I'm not guessing. I worked on data systems at Warner Bros and Discovery — petabyte-scale media. Then Deutsche Telekom — the biggest brand in Germany. Now I'm in Netherlands working for the largest telecom provider in Switzerland. Every architecture decision in this course is one I've actually shipped or debugged in production. No theory.

### Framing B — "Skills compound"
> 2018: Infosys mainframes. 2020: Warner Bros & Discovery, working on data engineering. 2022: Deutsche Telekom, Germany — building real ML systems. 2024: Switzerland's largest telecom, on Agentic AI. Each step looks like a leap, but it wasn't — it was the same skills, compounded year over year. Foundations matter. That's why this roadmap starts with `Python + async`, not LangChain.

### Framing C — "Global companies, one playbook"
> Different countries, different domains — media, telecom, fintech-adjacent — and one thing stays constant: the engineering playbook. Build small. Show errors. Measure before optimizing. Document for the next person. The companies and salaries change. The discipline doesn't. That's what I'm teaching.

## STORY_NETHERLANDS_NOW — Living in Europe, working remote for Swiss telecom

**One-liner**: Right now I'm in Netherlands, working for a company in Switzerland. AI engineering removes borders.

### Framing A — "Remote-first, global pay"
> I'm recording this from Netherlands. The company I work for is in Switzerland — the largest telecom there. I've never *moved* to Switzerland. I work from a Dutch apartment, in Euros, on Swiss-telecom systems. This is what AI engineering looks like in 2026. The good roles are remote, global, and pay 5–10x what the same role pays in India. That's the upside you're working toward.

### Framing B — "Why I can do this channel from here"
> I'm in Netherlands. India is 3.5 hours ahead. So when I record videos at 11 PM my time, you watch them at 2 AM IST — perfect for the next morning's commute or college class. The schedule isn't an accident. It's designed around where I actually live and where you actually watch.

### Framing C — "This is the door upskilling opens"
> I want to be honest. I'm not going to promise you'll move to Europe. But I will tell you what's actually possible: I went from `3 LPA` at Infosys to working for a Swiss telecom — physically based in Netherlands — in 6 years. The skills in this roadmap are the door. Whether you walk through it is on you.

## STORY_AFTER_FULLTIME — Building this after a full-time job

**One-liner**: I record, edit, script, manage social — all after a full-time job and gym. Free course = your discipline.

### Framing A — "Effort symmetry"
> Quick math. I have a full-time job at a Swiss telecom. Then gym. Then freelance teaching. Then I script videos. Then I record. Then I edit. Then I read every comment and reply. That's my evening. If I'm putting that in — for free — I expect *something* from your side. Not money. Just effort. Watch the videos, build the projects, ask doubts. That's the trade.

### Framing B — "Free course needs more discipline, not less"
> "It's free" sounds great until week 3. Because no one is taking attendance. No one is failing you. There's no fee receipt making you feel guilty for skipping. So free course actually needs *more* discipline than a paid one. If you're someone who quits the moment something gets boring, I'd genuinely tell you to pay for a course — the sunk cost will at least force you to finish.

### Framing C — "Why I do it for free"
> Why am I doing this for free? One reason. In 2018, when I was lost on Infosys mainframes, one person — Srikanth Verma — gave me a roadmap. Not a job. Not money. Just a clear path. That changed my career. I'm not building a paid course right now. I'm trying to be that person, for whoever's watching.
